## 1. Targeted vs anti-targeted word dropout at matched drop rate: testing whether column subsampling helps by breaking reliance on a few topic tokens

We transfer XGBoost-style column subsampling to a bag-of-embeddings MLP as per-batch vocabulary-token dropout. The goal is to test why it helps, not whether it helps. The core comparison holds the expected number of dropped tokens per document fixed and varies which tokens are dropped. Uniform: tokens are dropped at random. Targeted: drop probability is proportional to each token's current class-discriminative saliency, defined as |gradient x embedding| toward the true-class logit and recomputed every epoch from a running estimate. Anti-targeted: the least salient tokens are dropped at the same rate. L1 shrinkage on embedding rows serves as the second boosting-style arm. Hidden dropout plus weight decay is the reference regularizer. If H1 is right, the gains come from the uniform and targeted arms, and anti-targeted dropout behaves like plain noise injection with little benefit. Mechanism read-outs are logit-margin concentration, measured as the share of the margin carried by the top-3 tokens, and error after deleting each validation document's top-k salient tokens. All arms get equal per-arm tuning (P2) and are evaluated at their own best epoch. This controls for H2, the possibility that the gain is early stopping in disguise.

- **Direction:** Boosting-style regularizers transferred to a neural bag-of-words...
- **Follows your steer:** This tests hunch H1 directly with a matched control (anti-targeted dropout at the same noise level) and handles H2 through per-arm best-epoch evaluation and an epochs-tuned reference. The author learns whether column subsampling's benefit is about token dominance, generic noise or merely stopping later. A null result on T-vs-A would still discriminate between these explanations.
- **Gap:** Gap 4 (whether explicit regularizers substitute for or stack with each other in small BoW models, untested on text) and the boosting-regularizer transfer implied by gap 2. Within these, the open question is the mechanism behind the gain from input-column subsampling.
- **Hypothesis:** Word-level column subsampling lowers val error mainly by reducing the model's dependence on a few highly discriminative tokens. It does not help through generic input noise. So at matched expected drop rate, targeted and uniform dropout should beat anti-targeted dropout. Their advantage should also coincide with lower margin concentration and better robustness to top-token deletion. → *Each arm is tuned by itself and evaluated at its best epoch, averaged over 10 seeds. Prediction: uniform and targeted dropout each reach at least 0.7 points lower val_top1_err than the tuned hidden-dropout+wd reference. Targeted should reach roughly 12.0–12.5 against a reference of about 13.0–13.3. Anti-targeted dropout should close less than one third of that gap. On the mechanism side, the top-3 margin share should fall by at least 10 percentage points under targeted and uniform dropout. Error after deleting the top-3 salient tokens should improve by at least 3 points relative to the reference and the anti-targeted arm. L1 on embedding rows should raise margin concentration, because sparsity favours few tokens. Any error gain from L1 should therefore not come with improved top-token-deletion robustness.*
- **Experiment:** Editable surfaces only: augmentation (token dropout variants), regularization (L1 on embedding rows, hidden dropout, weight_decay), and schedule (epochs, and evaluation at the best epoch). embed_dim, hidden and depth stay at their defaults (64/64/1) in every arm.

Arms: (R) hidden dropout + weight_decay; (U) uniform token dropout with rate p; (T) saliency-proportional token dropout with expected rate p; (A) inverse-saliency token dropout with expected rate p; (L) L1 on embedding rows with coefficient lambda; (U+R) a stacking check.

Saliency for T and A is computed per (vocabulary token, class) as an EMA of |grad x emb| on training batches and refreshed each epoch. During the first epoch, before saliency is estimated, T and A fall back to uniform dropout. Probabilities are clipped to [0, 0.9] and renormalized so that the expected number of dropped tokens per document equals p times the document length, which holds the noise amount fixed across arms.

Tuning follows the P2 estimand: 16 random-search trials per arm over lr, weight_decay, the arm's own strength (p or lambda or hidden dropout) and epochs (up to 16), each scored on 1 seed. The best config per arm is then rerun with 10 seeds. Budget: 6 arms x (16 + 10) = 156 runs, each a few seconds to a minute on a BoW MLP with 10k documents. Ample room remains for the deletion test.

To control for H2, record the full per-epoch val curve for every arm. Report error at each arm's best epoch and the epoch at which that best occurs. Additionally retune R with epochs as a free knob, so that a pure early-stopping effect is available to the reference arm.

Mechanism metrics, computed on validation: the top-3 token share of the logit margin, and error after deleting the top-k salient tokens (k = 1, 3, 5), with saliency computed from each arm's own model. As a sanity check, also report error after deleting k random tokens.

Statistics: paired-seed bootstrap confidence intervals for T-vs-A, U-vs-A and U-vs-R. Compare all arms against the 13.3 logistic-regression baseline.

## 2. Informativeness-targeted vs anti-targeted word dropout at matched drop rate: does word dropout work by breaking reliance on topic-keyword shortcuts?

Word dropout on bag-of-embeddings models is known prior work (e.g., the deep averaging network line of work). It is also the per-example analogue of XGBoost's column subsampling [arXiv:1603.02754]. Its mechanism is rarely isolated. The author's hunch H1 says it helps by stopping the model from leaning on a few topic-specific tokens, not by adding generic noise. This idea tests that directly with three arms matched on the expected number of tokens dropped per document. (a) Uniform word dropout. (b) Targeted dropout, where each token's drop probability is proportional to its class-discriminativeness. Discriminativeness is the max over classes of the smoothed log-odds or chi-square score, computed once on the 10k labelled training split. (c) Anti-targeted dropout, where the probability is proportional to the inverse score, so mostly uninformative tokens are dropped. Per document, the probabilities are renormalised so that the expected count of dropped tokens equals the uniform rate p. The arms then differ in which tokens are removed, not how many. H1 predicts targeted ≥ uniform > anti-targeted. The generic-noise account predicts the three arms are indistinguishable at matched p. A reliance-concentration probe measures the share of the logit margin carried by each document's top-3 tokens, plus error when those tokens are masked at validation. This probe tests whether the gains travel with reduced shortcut reliance. Every arm is scored both at the final epoch and at its best epoch, so that the gain is not early stopping in disguise (H2).

- **Follows your steer:** This idea tests H1 directly, using a matched control (anti-targeted dropout at an equal drop count). Best-epoch scoring also checks H2, so the author learns whether word dropout's benefit is shortcut suppression, generic noise, or later peaking.
- **Gap:** Open gap 4 (how explicit regularizers act in small BoW models, which no reviewed paper studies on text), restricted to the regularization and augmentation surfaces, together with the column-subsampling transfer from gap 2 [arXiv:1603.02754]

Removing class-discriminative tokens regularizes more than removing uninformative ones. The gain is mediated by lower concentration of the decision margin on a few tokens. → *Each arm uses its own tuned p. Targeted dropout should beat anti-targeted by at least 1.0 point of val_top1_err, and uniform should sit in between. The ranking should hold at both final-epoch and best-epoch scoring. Targeted and uniform runs should show a top-3 margin share at least 15% lower (relative) than the no-dropout baseline. The masked-top-3 validation error gap should also shrink. If anti-targeted is within noise of uniform, which is 2 SE across 8 seeds, H1 is not supported.*

Precompute per-token discriminativeness on the 10k training split only, then implement the three dropout samplers with per-document renormalisation to an expected drop count of p·len(doc). Keep architecture knobs at their defaults (embed_dim 64, hidden 64, depth 1) throughout. Arms: (0) no dropout; (1) uniform; (2) targeted; (3) anti-targeted; (4) the baseline's hidden dropout plus weight decay. P2 tuning [arXiv:2611.14508v1, arXiv:2611.14519v1] gives each arm its own 16-trial random search over lr ∈ [1e-3, 1e-2], weight_decay ∈ [1e-5, 1e-3], epochs ∈ {8, 12, 16} and p ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5} where applicable, all on a held-out slice of train. ## 3. Salience-targeted versus anti-targeted vocabulary-column dropout at matched drop rate: a direct test of H1

Here XGBoost's per-tree column subsampling becomes per-batch vocabulary-column dropout in a bag-of-embeddings classifier. For each minibatch, a random subset of vocabulary ids is zeroed in every document of the batch. This differs from i.i.d. per-token word dropout, where each token occurrence is dropped independently. The mechanism test compares three ways of choosing which columns to drop, all at the same expected fraction of dropped token occurrences: (a) uniform, (b) salience-targeted, with drop probability proportional to each token's training-set class-conditional max-PMI, so topic-specific tokens are dropped most often, and (c) anti-targeted, with low-salience tokens dropped most often. If H1 holds, the benefit comes from denying the model its few topic-defining tokens. Then (b) should be at least as good as (a), and (a) should beat (c). If word dropout is generic noise, all three arms should tie. A probe measures reliance concentration directly: the error increase when the top-k PMI tokens of each validation document are masked at test time.

- **Direction:** Boosting-style regularizers transferred to a neural bag-of-words...
- **Follows your steer:** This acts on hunch H1 and, through the best-epoch reporting, on H2. The author learns whether word-level dropout helps by suppressing topic-specific tokens: a positive targeted-versus-anti-targeted gap with lower masking sensitivity supports H1, and a tie is a clean negative.
- **Gap:** Open gap 4 asks how explicit regularizers interact with model capacity in small bag-of-words MLPs, and no reviewed paper tests why input-level subsampling helps text bag-of-words models. This idea attacks the regularizer-mechanism half of that gap, together with the transfer of [arXiv:1603.02754]'s column subsampling to a neural bag-of-words model. It uses per-arm tuning (P2) as recommended by [arXiv:2611.14508v1].
- **Hypothesis:** At a matched expected token-drop rate, salience-targeted column dropout beats anti-targeted column dropout on val_top1_err. The gap tracks a reduction in test-time sensitivity to masking the top-k salient tokens. This means word-level dropout works by spreading reliance away from topic-specific tokens, not by acting as generic noise. → *Targeted minus anti-targeted val_top1_err is at most -1.0 points (mean over 5 seeds, each arm with its own tuned drop rate). The targeted arm's top-5-mask error increase is at least 30% smaller than the baseline's. Under the noise view, the arm differences fall below 0.4 points and the masking-sensitivity differences are not significant.*
- **Experiment:** Edit only the augmentation and regularization code in the training script. Compute each token's PMI with the classes once, on the 10k training subset only, never on validation. Arms: (0) the baseline, hidden dropout plus weight decay, tuned. (1) i.i.d. per-token word dropout. (2) Uniform per-batch column dropout. (3) Salience-targeted column dropout. (4) Anti-targeted column dropout. In arms 2-4, per-token probabilities are rescaled so the expected fraction of dropped occurrences equals a target rate r. Per arm, run a P2 search with an equal budget of 12 trials over r ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5} (or the baseline's dropout and weight decay) crossed with lr ∈ {0.001, 0.003, 0.01}. Keep embed_dim=64, hidden=64, depth=1 and epochs=8 fixed. Rerun each arm's best configuration with 5 seeds. Report the final-epoch error, the best-epoch error and the epoch at which each run peaks; this is the H2 control. Also report the masking-probe curves for k = 1, 3, 5. Cost: 5 arms × 12 trials plus 5 arms × 5 seeds, about 85 short runs, which is trivial for a bag-of-words model on 10k documents. The comparison targets are the tuned baseline arm and the 13.3 logistic-regression bar.
- **Builds on:** `arXiv:1603.02754`, `arXiv:2611.14508v1`, `arXiv:2611.14519v1`
- **Why novel (agent):** Word dropout itself is known: it appears in the DAN paper by Iyyer et al. (2015), and column subsampling is standard in XGBoost [arXiv:1603.02754]. Neither is claimed as new. What is new is the matched-rate targeted-versus-anti-targeted contrast, paired with a masking probe that tests the reliance-spreading mechanism directly. We did not find this contrast in the reviewed papers, though absence from the review does not establish novelty. Salience-guided masking does exist in other forms, so a novelty search is needed.

- **Mechanism:** Dropping high-PMI tokens forces the hidden layer to use co-occurring weaker evidence. This lowers the variance that comes from memorizing rare, discriminative tokens in 10k labels.
- **Intervention:** Per-batch vocabulary-column dropout. The column-selection distribution is uniform, salience-weighted or inverse-salience-weighted, with the expected drop rate matched across arms.
